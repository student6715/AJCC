\textbf{\Large Q1. Mate in 2. (4 marks)}\\
{\setchessboard{boardfontsize=30pt,labelfontsize=14pt}
\newchessgame
\chessboard[setfen=8/7R/k1Kp4/3N4/r7/2Q5/8/8 w - - 0 1]}\\
\textbf{\Large Answer:}\\
\variation{1. Qd3+ Rc4 2. Qxc4+ Ka4 3. Qb4#} takes 3 moves. The right answer starts from Queen sacrifice, \variation{1. Qa5+ Rxa5 2. Nb4#}. If Black King takes White Queen, \variation{1... Kxa5 2.Ra7#}.\\

\textbf{\Large It is very important that you can only get the point when you answer the checkmate moves with a given moves (in this question, 2 moves, not 3 moves.)}\\

\vfill{\Large Source: Jeff Coakley, Winning Chess Puzzles for Kids Vol. 2}

\textbf{\Large Q2. Mate in 2. (4 marks)}\\
{\setchessboard{boardfontsize=30pt,labelfontsize=14pt}
\newchessgame
\chessboard[setfen=kbK5/pp6/1P6/8/8/8/8/R7 w - - 0 1]}\\
\textbf{\Large Answer:}\\
This is one of the most famous and hard to solve "Mate in 2" puzzles. The supprising first move is \variation{1. Ra6!}. If the b7 Pawn takes the Rook, \variation{1... bxa6 2. b7#}. If the bishop moves, \variation{1... Bh2 2. Rxa7#}. The Bishop moves to any legal squares (c7, d6, e5, f4, g3) are also correct answers.

\vfill{\Large Source: Paul Morphy, 1910 (YACDB: 625426)}

\noindent\textbf{\Large Q5. What is the missing piece? Which turns? What is the position of the missing piece? Then, answer the last few moves.}\\
{\setchessboard{boardfontsize=30pt, labelfontsize=14pt, showmover=false}
\newchessgame
\chessboard[setfen=8/8/8/1r1b4/B7/8/8/3k4 - - 0 1]}\\

\textbf{\Large Answer:}\\
Article 1.2 of "Laws of Chess" says "Leaving one's own king under attack, exposing one's own king to attack and also 'capturing' the opponent's king are not allowed."\\
1. King can not be captured, so the one missing piece is White king.\\
2. Black king is in check by White Bishop. You can not leave your king under attack. Therefore, Black's move now.\\
3. What is the previous White's move? It is not White's Bishop move, so White King moved from b3 to c3, which creates discovery check to Black King.
4.

\textbf{\Large -1. Kb3-Kxc3+}\\
\textbf{\Large -2. c2-c4 -Bd5+}\\
\variation{1...Bd5+ 2. c4 bxc3+ 3. Kxc3+}\\
This puzzle was used in 2019 Q10.\\

\vfill{\Large Source: Raymond M. Smullyan: The Chess Mysteries of the Arabian Knights}

\noindent\textbf{\Large Q6. WHO moved last? WHICH piece was moved from WHERE to WHERE?}\\
{\setchessboard{boardfontsize=30pt, labelfontsize=14pt, showmover=false}
\newchessgame
\chessboard[setfen=rnR1kbnr/pp2pppp/3p4/1B4N1/1R1BP3/3P2P1/PPP2PP1/3Q1KN1 b kq - 0 1]}\\

\textbf{\Large Answer:}\\
Black king in now in check, actually under double check from Rc8 and Bb5. It is illeagal to move into check or leave your king in check. Therefore, the last move is made by White. Double check always come from discovery check and check by that move. The only possible move is a move of Rook from c6 to c8.


This puzzle was used in 2024 Q1.\\

\vfill{\Large Source: Noam Elkies, U.S. Problem Bulletin, 1995}

\textbf{\Large Q6. Proof game. The position below occured after the Black's 4th move. Can you answer the moves.}\\

{\setchessboard{boardfontsize=30pt,labelfontsize=14pt}
\newchessgame
\chessboard[setfen=rnbqkbnr/pp3ppp/2p1p3/8/4P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNBQK1NR]}\\

\textbf{\Large Answer:}\\
This type of chess problem is called "Proof game", a type of retrograde analysis. This position is easily reached by \variation{1. e4 e6 2. Bb5 c6 3. Bxc6 dxc6}. However, this problem is asking the same position after Black's 4th move. There should be illogical, but legal White and Black move. The answer is \variation{1. e4 e6 2. Bb5+ Ke7 3. Bxd7 c6 4. Be8 Kxe8}.

\vfill{\Large Source: Tibor Orbán, Die Schwalbe, 1976}
